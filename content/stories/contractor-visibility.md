Texas's technology contracting architecture is extensive and sophisticated at the vendor level. DIR maintains statewide purchasing vehicles, approved vendor lists, standard solicitations, staff categories, not-to-exceed rates, HUB subcontracting plans and statement-of-work review processes.

But when it comes to the individual workers actually performing taxpayer-funded labor, the picture becomes far less clear.

## What the System Sees

Current DIR materials state that ITSAC customers may competitively solicit through requests for resumes or SOWs. For state-agency technology SOWs over $50,000, DIR reviews the SOW before solicitation and reviews/signs the final SOW before it becomes valid and money is paid.

The current-generation Appendix E staffing template asks whether all work is expected to be remote and asks what workspace/equipment the agency and vendor will provide. It contains cybersecurity obligations for vendor employees, officers and subcontractors accessing state systems.

## What the System Doesn't See

In the two current templates independently reviewed for this project, **no dedicated field was found for citizenship, immigration/work-authorization category or offshore delivery.** The staffing table focuses on labor category, title/level, estimated hours and rate.

The underlying RFO states that hourly bill rates include wages, benefits, fees, usual living expenses and commuting to the customer's designated primary work site. That shows the procurement model can recognize a worksite, but it does not by itself create public reporting of each remote worker's actual geography.

## The Transaction Chain

Follow one technology purchase through the system:

| Stage | What Texas Records | What Remains Unknown |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **DIR Vehicle** | Contract number, vendor, term, job categories, NTE rates | Who receives the assignment? |
| **Agency SOW** | Role, skills, hours, rate, sometimes remote/locality | Where will the worker physically work? |
| **Purchase Order** | Vendor, authorization, ceiling, dates | Prime employee or subcontractor? |
| **Payment** | Vendor, amount, date | Which worker earned this payment? |
| **Worker** | Sometimes résumé in agency file | Texas resident? Actual geography? Visa status? |

## Proof That Texas Can Ask

The evidence also contains an important qualification. Current 2026 HHSC solicitations reproduced by multiple staffing vendors impose Texas-locality requirements for some positions:

- One hybrid Software Engineer 3 request requires the candidate to be **local to the Austin area**
- Another remote PeopleSoft request is consistently reproduced as allowing candidates **anywhere within Texas**

A Pedigo Staffing posting for the PeopleSoft solicitation says candidates must be U.S. citizens, green-card holders or EAD holders and states "No H1B." That is presently a vendor/recruiter record, not a primary HHSC solicitation — it should not be attributed to HHSC until the original is obtained.

**The existence of agency-specific controls strengthens the governance question:** if worker geography is material enough to be specified in individual solicitations, why is it not standardized into a public statewide labor ledger?

## The Digerati/TWC Case

Digerati Systems illustrates why company-level records cannot answer a worker-level policy promise.

The company's current Texas procurement footprint includes ITSAC and DBITS contract vehicles. The internal case file identifies a TWC staff-augmentation award with a **$363,377 ceiling** dated February 6, 2026 — shortly after Abbott's H-1B directive. The role: Business Analyst 2 work supporting unemployment-insurance data exchange.

The working case file separately records certified LCAs naming Texas DIR and TxDOT as secondary entities in earlier periods. Those LCAs do not establish that the workers started, were paid through the identified public contract or remained on those assignments.

**The case demonstrates an information asymmetry rather than an H-1B finding.** Public records can identify a vendor, category, rate, hours and payment. They do not necessarily tell the public whether the individual worker was a Texas resident or what work-authorization category applied.
