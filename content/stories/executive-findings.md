## The question Abbott asked — and the one Texas cannot answer

On January 27, 2026, Gov. Greg Abbott issued a directive built around a promise almost impossible to misunderstand: taxpayer-funded employment opportunities should be filled by Texans first.

Abbott ordered Texas state agencies and public universities to freeze new H-1B visa petitions unless the Texas Workforce Commission approved them. His letter described the federal program as vulnerable to abuse by employers that replace American workers with lower-cost foreign labor. State government, he wrote, had to lead by example.

There is an obvious question hiding inside that declaration.

**Who is actually performing the work Texas taxpayers buy?**

For direct state employment, Texas can begin to answer. Agencies know whom they employ. They know whether they petition for an H-1B worker. Abbott's directive asks them to report that activity.

But Texas does not obtain all of its workforce directly. It buys enormous quantities of technology labor through private companies. The state has built reusable procurement vehicles through the Department of Information Resources. Agencies, universities, local governments, schools and other eligible entities can use those vehicles to purchase programmers, architects, analysts, cloud engineers, cybersecurity specialists and other technical labor.

The public records can be extraordinarily precise about the transaction. They can identify the agency, the vendor, the labor category, the hourly rate, the purchase order and the dollars paid.

What Texas has not assembled is the corresponding public labor ledger: the prime and subcontractor chain; the authorized location where the work was actually performed; the material offshore component; and aggregate categories showing whether taxpayer-funded labor was supplied by citizens, permanent residents, temporary-visa workers or workers outside the United States.

That missing ledger matters because Abbott did not merely make employment an administrative issue. He made it a principle of state policy.

**If taxpayer-funded jobs are supposed to go to Texans first, Texas needs to know who is doing the taxpayer-funded work.**

## Abbott's own numbers change the scale of the question

The most revealing numbers in this investigation do not come from an immigration advocacy group, an opposition campaign or an estimate assembled from visa filings. They come from Abbott's own economic-development office.

A Texas Economic Development & Tourism Office profile revised in July 2025 reports that, from January 2015 through December 2024, Indian companies announced 74 investment projects in Texas, $2.7 billion in capital investment and more than 8,650 new jobs.

During the same period, the same state profile says Texas companies announced 175 investment projects in India, $7.6 billion in capital investment and more than 59,300 new jobs.

| 2015–2024 Reported FDI | India → Texas | Texas → India |
|------------------------|---------------|---------------|
| Projects | 74 | 175 |
| Announced capital | $2.7B | $7.6B |
| Announced jobs | 8,650+ | 59,300+ |

Using the Governor's own figures, Texas-company projects in India outnumber Indian-company projects in Texas by more than two to one. Announced capital runs almost three to one. Announced jobs run almost seven to one.

Those figures are not a balance sheet. Foreign direct investment is not money simply packed into a suitcase and removed from Texas. A Texas-headquartered multinational can finance an Indian expansion with global capital. Announced jobs are not the same as verified payroll. Indian investment in Texas can create supplier activity, tax revenue and other benefits that a simple project count does not capture.

But those caveats do not erase what the numbers measure: the relationship Texas spent a decade promoting was dramatically larger in the Texas-company-to-India direction on the very metrics economic-development officials use to celebrate success.

There is another reason to examine the numbers closely. During Abbott's January 2024 India mission, his office repeatedly said Indian companies had invested $1.4 billion through 59 Texas projects and created more than 10,300 jobs, citing Indian industry estimates. The later state profile reports more projects and almost twice as much capital — but fewer jobs, 8,650-plus — using fDi Markets.

That may be a methodological difference. It may reflect different periods, definitions or project databases. But jobs are the benefit Abbott's office repeatedly sold to Texans. The state should be able to reconcile the two claims company by company.

**Texas has been very good at counting announcements. The harder question is what it counts after the ribbon is cut.**

## Texas built the bridge deliberately

The Texas-India economic relationship was not an accident of globalization that happened around state government. Texas government and its public-private economic-development partners deliberately cultivated it.

Abbott led economic-development missions to India in 2018 and again in 2024. His office described the purpose of the later trip as encouraging trade, job creation and capital investment by Indian companies in Texas. The Texas Economic Development Corporation — an independently funded nonprofit that markets Texas alongside the Governor's Economic Development & Tourism Office — sponsored and paid for the 2024 mission.

The 2024 delegation included Secretary of State Jane Nelson, economic-development director Adriana Cruz, TxEDC vice chairman Arun Agarwal and TxEDC president Aaron Demerson. Abbott met executives and officials across Mumbai and New Delhi and highlighted technology repeatedly.

His office's own releases document meetings or visits involving Tata Consultancy Services and HCLTech and describe earlier engagement with Wipro. The Governor framed Texas and India as economic partners positioned to lead the future of technology.

The 2018 trip was similarly consequential. Abbott visited Wipro's Bengaluru campus and announced a new Wipro Texas Technology Center in Plano. David Whitley — then one of Abbott's senior officials and later Texas secretary of state — traveled on that mission.

None of this is inherently suspect. Governors recruit companies. States compete for investment. Texas has every reason to sell itself as a destination for global capital.

**The accountability problem appears when the state celebrates the inbound side of a global labor-and-capital relationship without maintaining an equally rigorous accounting of the labor and economic value moving through the contractor market it created at home.**

## At the other end of the bridge, Andhra Pradesh was building machinery to capture the value

The Indian side of this story becomes clearer when the institutions are allowed to describe themselves.

The Andhra Pradesh Non-Resident Telugu Society, or APNRTS, is a Government of Andhra Pradesh institution created to organize and serve the state's global Telugu diaspora. Its current investment operation does considerably more than cultural outreach.

Official Andhra materials describe an Investments Wing that assists nonresident investors seeking to establish businesses or offshore operations. The state's investment portal describes a pipeline that connects prospective investors to policies and incentives, government officials, site selection, land, approvals and aftercare.

Its coordinator rules likewise treat overseas representatives as economic-development infrastructure. Coordinators promote Andhra investment, connect the government with diaspora organizations and prospective investors, and are expected to help bring investment opportunities home.

That language matters because it tells us what success looked like from Andhra's perspective. The objective was not simply a stronger relationship with Texas or the United States. The objective was to convert overseas relationships into operations, investment and employment inside Andhra Pradesh.

That is ordinary economic development. It is also a useful contrast.

**Texas marketed the bridge. Andhra built machinery to capture value at its end.**

## ITServe companies were invited onto that machinery

ITServe Alliance is an association of technology consulting and staffing companies founded in the United States. In Washington, its public identity has been unusually direct: educate, lobby, litigate. It organized congressional outreach, immigration advocacy and litigation involving the third-party consulting model.

But its members also appear on the other side of the bridge.

Contemporaneous 2016 reporting on Andhra's recruitment effort says ITServe member companies signed memoranda to establish operations in the state. Reports described government support that included plug-and-play office space, discounted rent, hiring incentives, workforce-development assistance and access to government technology opportunities.

One account described 50 ITServe member companies signing MOUs. Another described Andhra officials responding to the practical concerns of U.S.-based IT consulting companies about opening Indian offices.

The exact subsidy received by each company still requires the underlying government orders, MOUs and disbursement records. An offered incentive is not the same thing as a paid subsidy.

**But the policy direction is not ambiguous. A U.S.-based industry whose members sold technology labor was being actively recruited by an Indian state that explicitly wanted investment, offices and jobs.**

## Dallas Technology Centre made the bridge physical

The Dallas Technology Centre is one of the most important records in the investigation because it turns an abstract network into a physical project.

Official Andhra Pradesh Industrial Infrastructure Corporation records show a 2015 approval for Dallas Technology Centre at the IT non-SEZ Hill 2 site in Visakhapatnam.

Contemporaneous ITServe-related reporting described DTC as a roughly ₹192-crore project planned by ITServe member firms and identified intended occupants including IntelliSoft, Primus, Kairos, Voxai and Qatalys.

Those names matter because several of the firms or principals have U.S. and Texas footprints. But planned occupancy is not proof that each company ultimately occupied the building. That is one of the records still being reconstructed.

What is established is the larger sequence: U.S.-linked technology entrepreneurs organized around an Indian state-backed technology project; Andhra allotted land; and years later the facility became part of another major technology expansion.

In late 2024, Andhra approved Dallas Technology Centre to sublease its Rushikonda premises to Tata Consultancy Services and considered a special package to facilitate the company's operations. In 2025, Andhra separately approved land in Visakhapatnam for a proposed TCS development center expected to create more than 12,000 jobs.

**One proposed TCS project in Andhra was therefore associated with more announced jobs than the 8,650-plus jobs the Texas Governor's later profile attributes to all Indian-company FDI projects in Texas over the preceding decade.**

Those are not apples-to-apples realized-job figures, and they should not be presented as such. But they illustrate the economic-development objective at the other end of the relationship with unusual clarity: land, operating capacity, investment and employment.

## The same market kept meeting Texas government

Back in Texas, technology consulting was not merely a private export industry. It was also a public market.

DIR's Cooperative Contracts program gives thousands of eligible public customers access to statewide technology contracts. A 2024 Sunset review reported approximately 800 COOP contracts and $3.4 billion in fiscal-year 2023 sales. ITSAC — Information Technology Staff Augmentation Contracts — sits inside that ecosystem and supplies temporary IT personnel on an hourly basis.

That distinction matters. Abbott's H-1B directive governs petitions filed by covered state entities. A private staffing or technology company is a different employer. A state agency can therefore buy labor from a private vendor without itself being the H-1B petitioner.

That does not mean the contractor is using an H-1B worker. It means the petition freeze and the procurement channel are different systems.

Texas's public data reflects that separation. Procurement records can identify what was bought without identifying the labor category Abbott made politically important.

## One worker slot explains the entire accounting problem

The Texas Workforce Commission provides a useful example precisely because the records do not prove an immigration violation.

In late 2025, TWC sought a Business Analyst 2 for an unemployment-insurance data-exchange project. Digerati Systems submitted a candidate. TWC approved the candidate on January 7, 2026, with a planned January 12 start.

Abbott issued his H-1B directive on January 27.

On February 6, TWC issued purchase order 26-0002508 authorizing 3,700 hours at $98.21 per hour, with a maximum value of $363,377. Subsequent Comptroller payments reviewed in this investigation totaled $74,639.60 — exactly 760 hours at the purchase-order rate.

The solicitation said TWC does not sponsor visas for staff-augmentation contractors and required work eligibility.

The candidate's name is redacted in the public material. The worker's immigration category is not published. This investigation has not found evidence that the worker was on H-1B.

**That is the point.**

Texas can publish the customer, vendor, job classification, rate, authorized hours, purchase order and cash. But it does not publish the aggregate labor fact Abbott elevated into state policy.

If the state wants to claim that taxpayer-funded employment is going to Texans first, 'the vendor handled it' is not an accounting system.

## Texas had been warned about contractor oversight long before 2026

The contractor blind spot did not emerge in a system that had never been audited.

In March 2016, the State Auditor examined selected agencies' use of DIR information-technology staffing contracts. DSHS and TWC generally complied with applicable requirements, but auditors identified areas where contract-management controls should be strengthened.

Among them, auditors found that the agencies lacked procedures sufficient to verify vendors' compliance with then-existing E-Verify requirements for personnel assigned to state contracts. The report also addressed conflicts, vendor performance and implementation of earlier DIR recommendations.

The distribution list matters. The report was sent to the Office of the Governor, naming Greg Abbott, and to DIR's governing board, naming then-chair John B. Scott.

Distribution does not prove Abbott personally read every page. It establishes institutional notice during his first term.

The same report found DIR had fully implemented only 18 of 33 recommendations from an earlier cooperative-contract audit. Seven were substantially implemented, six were incomplete or ongoing and two had not been implemented.

The warnings continued, though their subjects changed.

In 2021, the State Auditor gave a High rating to weaknesses in DIR's vendor-scoring process for a major cloud procurement. Auditors found inconsistent scoring justifications and criteria, incomplete documentation and disclosure/training problems. The audit also found that DIR had complied with statutory planning and procurement requirements overall. Both facts matter.

The 2024 Sunset review was similarly balanced. It credited DIR with improvements and said recent audits had not produced priority contracting findings. But Sunset still found statewide IT procurement capability gaps, inconsistent COOP pricing information and a need for stronger vendor-compliance checks. It specifically discussed ITSAC as an active staff-augmentation market.

The contract-monitoring assessments became broader still. In April 2025, the State Auditor concluded that nine of Texas's 25 largest agencies warranted additional contract monitoring for at least one contracting period. The list included TWC, HHSC, DSHS and TxDOT — agencies that also appear in the state's technology-contracting ecosystem.

Then, in June 2026 — months after Abbott's H-1B directive — the State Auditor reported that 13 of the 25 largest agencies, 52 percent, warranted additional contract monitoring for at least one contracting period.

Those ratings are not immigration findings. They do not prove offshore work, H-1B abuse or misconduct by a technology vendor. **They establish something more basic: contract oversight remained a live governance problem in the same year Texas declared a Texans-first employment policy.**

## The revolving door raises a separate judgment question

The records also show former senior Texas officials moving into paid representation of companies operating in the technology market.

David Whitley is a particularly useful example because the primary lobbying record is concrete. Whitley served Abbott in senior roles, traveled on the 2018 India mission and later served as secretary of state.

A 2020 Texas Ethics Commission lobbyist roster shows Whitley representing Wipro beginning in February of that year and, beginning June 26, both Accenture and Nextt Affiliated. Nextt is associated with Arun Agarwal, who co-chaired Abbott's 2018 India mission and later rose to lead TxEDC.

The significance is not that sharing a lobbyist proves the companies coordinated. It does not. Nor does a state lobbying registration establish that Whitley influenced a municipal procurement such as Austin's later Workday implementation.

**The significance is structural: a former senior Abbott official became a paid intermediary for multiple technology and India-facing business interests that operated around the same broader public market.**

John B. Scott presents a different version of the same question. Abbott appointed Scott to chair DIR in 2016. Abbott's own later biography credited Scott with helping develop strategic technology and security planning for executive-branch agencies. Scott subsequently became secretary of state and interim attorney general. Texas Ethics Commission records then show him registered for Deloitte Consulting in 2024, 2025 and 2026.

Deloitte is a major DIR vendor with multiple statewide technology vehicles, including current ITSAC and DBITS contracts.

Again, that does not prove Scott influenced a Deloitte award. The investigation has not established that.

**It raises a conventional public-integrity question: what revolving-door safeguards should apply when officials entrusted with understanding and shaping the state's technology environment later represent companies selling into it?**

## Abbott's judgment is now part of the story

There is no evidence in the records reviewed for this story that Greg Abbott secretly directed contracts to favored companies, ordered Texas work sent offshore or participated in a quid pro quo.

The accountability question is larger and more ordinary than that.

Abbott has governed Texas since January 2015 — essentially the same decade measured by his office's Texas-India investment profile. His administration aggressively promoted international technology investment. He personally led India missions. His economic-development apparatus celebrated jobs and capital. His senior officials occupied key points in the state's technology and economic-development institutions. Some later represented technology companies.

His administration also received repeated audit information about weaknesses in contracting, staffing, procurement and vendor oversight.

Then, in 2026, Abbott declared that taxpayer-funded employment opportunities should go to Texans first.

**After more than a decade, that makes the missing ledger a question of gubernatorial judgment.**

If Texas was going to build a globalized public-technology market, why didn't it build the accounting system necessary to measure whether the economic benefits its leaders promised were actually being retained in Texas?

If Andhra Pradesh could explicitly organize diaspora representatives to recruit investment, facilitate offshore operations, assemble land and incentives and count jobs, why did Texas settle for counting announcements and invoices?

And if the Governor now believes labor category is important enough to justify a statewide H-1B intervention, why is the same information absent from the contractor channel through which Texas purchases so much technology labor?

## This is not a story about nationality

The records do not support a claim that Indian immigrants as a group did something wrong. They do not show that every H-1B worker displaced an American. They do not show that every technology staffing company abuses immigration law. They do not show that political contributions bought contracts or that Texas officials conspired with Indian officials.

Nor would those claims be necessary to establish the accountability problem.

India and Andhra Pradesh are entitled to pursue their economic interests. Companies are entitled to organize, lobby, litigate, expand internationally and seek lawful incentives. Former officials may lawfully become lobbyists. Texas may lawfully recruit foreign investment and use private contractors.

**The relevant distinction is not Indian versus American.**

**It is measured versus unmeasured.**

One government built machinery explicitly designed to bring operations and jobs home. Texas built machinery to recruit companies and purchase services but did not create a public system that follows taxpayer-funded labor all the way through the vendor chain.

That is why the missing number is not merely a limitation of this investigation.

**It is one of its central findings.**

## Show us the ledger

Texas does not need to publish workers' names, immigration files or personal identifiers to answer the basic accountability question.

For public technology contracts, the state could require and publish the legal vendor family; prime and material subcontractors; statement-of-work labor locations; hours and rates; aggregate work-authorization categories; material offshore delivery; and any approved exceptions involving state data or overseas access.

When a federal labor filing identifies a Texas agency as an intended worksite or end client, the agency could confirm or dispute the relationship.

For economic-development awards, Texas could publish promised and verified jobs under stable definitions, amendments, payroll verification, clawbacks and closeouts — and distinguish between jobs actually located in Texas and broader corporate employment.

For TxEDC and state-supported missions, Texas could publish investor tiers, mission costs, project referrals, conflicts and recusals.

**None of that requires banning international trade or demonizing workers.**

**It requires ordinary government accounting.**

For a decade, Texas photographed the bridge. It counted the companies crossing it. It announced investment. It celebrated promised jobs. It awarded contracts and paid invoices.

At the other end, Andhra Pradesh openly built machinery to turn overseas relationships into investment, offices, offshore operations and employment.

Then Abbott said taxpayer-funded opportunities should go to Texans first.

Good.

**Now show Texans the ledger.**
