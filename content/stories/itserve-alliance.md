## ITServe shows what happens when a fragmented staffing industry learns to act collectively.

Technology staffing is structurally fragmented.

A state agency may see a list of vendors competing to supply a developer, analyst or architect. A worker may be employed by the prime contractor or by another company in a subcontract chain. Firms compete for customers, talent and margin while navigating immigration, tax, employment and procurement rules.

ITServe Alliance organized companies in that market around three verbs it has used to describe its work:

**Educate. Lobby. Litigate.**

Those verbs are more useful than any ethnic or political label.

They describe an industry association learning how to operate in a regulated labor market.

## Educate

ITServe built chapters, conferences and recurring programming for technology-business owners.

Its materials and events have addressed immigration compliance, recruiting, sales, contracting, taxes, employment law, cybersecurity, artificial intelligence and business development.

They also teach political participation.

Members meet lawmakers and staff, learn how policy affects the consulting model and advocate for their interests.

That is ordinary interest-group politics.

The organizational advantage is scale.

A small staffing company has limited leverage by itself. An association can aggregate many business owners across congressional districts and present a common policy concern.

Texas procurement encounters the companies one at a time.

The companies can encounter government as an industry.

## Lobby

High-skilled immigration policy has been economically important to a substantial portion of the technology-consulting market ITServe represents.

That statement needs precision.

An ITServe member is not necessarily H-1B dependent. An H-1B sponsor does not employ only visa workers. An LCA does not prove that a visa worker staffed a government contract.

But ITServe's advocacy history makes the industry interest unmistakable. Federal rules governing third-party placements, employer control, documentation and visa approval periods directly affected consulting companies whose employees work at customer sites.

ITServe responded with congressional outreach, professional advocacy and political organization.

Lawful campaign contributions are not evidence of a quid pro quo. Nor is meeting a member of Congress evidence of improper influence.

They are components of organized access — the same kind of access used by unions, professional associations, manufacturers, environmental organizations and nearly every mature industry.

## Litigate

When federal policy threatened the third-party consulting model, ITServe did more than lobby.

It sued.

The association became a named plaintiff in litigation challenging immigration policies affecting H-1B workers placed at customer sites, including policies concerning employer-employee relationships and shortened approval periods.

The litigation produced consequential relief for the industry.

Those victories should not be described as evidence that the business model was unlawful. Courts and settlements did not establish that ITServe members were collectively abusing the visa system.

The significance is institutional:

The association could identify a regulatory threat, organize affected companies, retain counsel, litigate against the federal government and alter the operating environment for its members.

That is what an effective trade association does.

## Texas was not incidental

ITServe was founded in the Dallas area, and North Texas became one of its important company clusters.

Texas politicians have appeared at ITServe events. Executives associated with the organization have made campaign contributions. Companies with ITServe ties appear in the broader Texas technology-vendor market.

Those facts should not be collapsed into a claim that ITServe controls procurement.

A company's membership does not prove that the association produced its contract. A political contribution does not prove a contract was purchased. A vendor's H-1B history does not identify the worker on a state assignment.

The useful observation is narrower:

An industry that Texas often sees as separate vendors built institutions for collective education, advocacy and litigation around the rules governing its labor model.

## The Andhra connection

The association also appears in the Andhra Pradesh recruitment story.

Contemporaneous reports described APNRTS and Andhra officials working with ITServe-associated companies interested in opening technology operations in the state. The same broad commercial community that cared about U.S. immigration rules could also care about Indian office space, incentives, workers and delivery capacity.

Those interests are not inherently contradictory.

A consulting company can want access to skilled workers in the United States, customers in Texas and delivery capacity in India.

That is precisely why Texas cannot use the corporate logo as a proxy for labor geography.

## Keep the categories separate

The Texas technology market contains different kinds of companies:

- small and midsize staffing firms;
- ITServe members and nonmembers;
- U.S.-headquartered integrators;
- Indian-headquartered global services firms;
- companies with heavy H-1B histories;
- companies with little or no visible visa activity;
- firms with offshore delivery centers;
- firms that primarily employ workers domestically.

They are not one category.

The investigation's working vendor census has found meaningful overlap among some of them, but the underlying datasets use different denominators and definitions. Publishing a single dramatic percentage before the methodology is mature would manufacture precision the records do not support.

Instead, the public-facing series uses harder facts: actual purchase orders, official contract architecture, official economic-development numbers and the exact fields Texas does not publish statewide.

The full census belongs in the evidence report with its methodology exposed.

## What organized industries understand

ITServe's history offers a lesson larger than ITServe.

A federal agency controls visa adjudication.

Congress writes immigration law.

Texas controls procurement.

A governor influences appointments and economic-development priorities.

Cities and universities buy services.

An Indian state controls its own incentives, land and approvals.

Sophisticated organizations operate where the relevant decision is made.

That is not infiltration.

It is advocacy.

The governance problem is that Texas has not organized its own contractor-labor information with equal sophistication.

The companies learned to see themselves as an industry.

Texas continued to see them as vendors.

The final question is what Texas should publish now.
