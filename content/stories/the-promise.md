## Texas said taxpayer-funded jobs should go to Texans. Its contractor records stop at the vendor.

On January 27, 2026, Gov. Greg Abbott made a promise that was unusually easy to understand.

"State government must lead by example," Abbott wrote, directing state agencies and public universities to freeze new H-1B visa petitions absent Texas Workforce Commission approval and to review their existing use of the program. Employment opportunities, he said, "particularly those funded with taxpayer dollars," should be filled by Texans first.

That directive governs a visible channel: public entities petitioning for H-1B workers themselves.

But Texas also buys labor.

Through the Department of Information Resources, state agencies and other eligible public entities can purchase developers, architects, analysts, project managers, cybersecurity specialists and other technology personnel from private companies. The current Information Technology Staff Augmentation Contracts program — ITSAC — uses competitively awarded vendors, standardized labor categories and set not-to-exceed rates. Agencies can request résumés or statements of work and issue purchase orders against those contracts.

The state can know the vendor. It can know the labor category. It can know the rate and authorized hours. It can impose security and contractual requirements on vendor personnel.

What the standard public-facing architecture does not consistently provide is the information Abbott's policy made politically important: Who actually performed the taxpayer-funded labor, and where?

That is not necessarily a legal loophole. It is an accounting boundary.

A private vendor is the employer. Texas is the customer. Federal immigration law remains federal law, and Abbott cannot rewrite it by executive directive. But Texas does control the terms on which it purchases services and the information it requires vendors to report.

If "Texans first" is meant to describe taxpayer-funded employment rather than only the state payroll, the distinction matters.

## A company is not a worker

A Texas company can employ someone in another state. An out-of-state company can employ a Texan. A prime contractor can subcontract the labor. A remote employee can work from Austin, Atlanta or another country.

A company with a large H-1B history can staff one Texas assignment entirely with U.S. citizens. A company with no obvious visa footprint can use a subcontractor that sponsors workers.

None of those facts can be inferred reliably from the vendor's name.

Yet vendor identity is where much of the public procurement record stops.

That is why this investigation does not attempt to label workers from company names, surnames or visa filings. A Labor Condition Application shows employer activity; it does not prove that a particular visa holder performed a particular state contract. DIR eligibility shows that a vendor may sell through a contract; it does not by itself establish spending. Corporate headquarters do not establish worker geography.

The problem is precisely that the state has not published the denominator needed to answer the question cleanly.

## The boundary is coherent. The rhetoric is broader.

There are legitimate reasons for Texas to treat direct employment and purchased services differently.

ITSAC exists because agencies need specialized technology labor, sometimes quickly and temporarily. Private vendors carry the employment relationship. Universities use H-1B workers in research, medicine and other labor markets that are not equivalent to a staff-augmentation developer or analyst. Individual immigration records also raise obvious privacy concerns.

None of that requires Texas to publish a worker's name, home address or immigration file.

It does mean the workable accountability question is aggregate and contractual:

- How many contractor labor hours did Texas buy?
- What share was performed by Texas residents?
- What share was performed outside Texas?
- Was any material work performed outside the United States?
- Which entity actually employed the worker?
- Was there a material subcontractor between the state's prime vendor and the person doing the work?
- What was the aggregate work-authorization mix of the contractor workforce?

Those are procurement questions as much as immigration questions.

## Texas already asks for location when it matters

The gap is not universal.

Records reviewed for this investigation include agency staffing requests that impose Texas-location requirements. Some hybrid requests require candidates to live within a defined distance of Austin. Other remote requests specify that work may be performed from anywhere within Texas.

That proves the administrative point: worker geography can be made a contract requirement when an agency chooses to make it one.

The statewide question is why the information is not standardized and reportable across the contractor system if the Governor believes taxpayer-funded worker geography matters.

Texas also demonstrated in 2026 that it can amend statewide ITSAC contracts when gubernatorial policy requires it. DIR contract pages show amendments implementing Abbott's separate foreign-adversary policy. India is not one of the countries targeted by that policy, and those amendments have nothing to do with H-1B visas. Their relevance is administrative: the statewide contracting machinery can carry a governor's policy into vendor terms.

The public record reviewed for this series has not shown a comparable statewide requirement that every material ITSAC assignment report Texas residency, actual work location, aggregate work-authorization category and material offshore delivery.

If such a system exists elsewhere, Texas should publish it.

If it does not, the state has made a political promise broader than its public accounting.

## What this series is — and is not — measuring

**This series is measuring:** the public accounting around technology labor Texas purchases from private contractors, with ITSAC as the clearest case study.

**This series is not measuring:** K-12 H-1B use; university research or medical H-1B employment as though it were ITSAC; private-sector visa use statewide; or whether any vendor violated immigration law.

## The question

The cleanest version of this investigation is not a claim that every contractor is foreign, every H-1B worker displaced a Texan, or every remote assignment left the state.

The records do not support those claims.

The finding is simpler:

Governor Abbott ordered Texas government to put Texans first in taxpayer-funded jobs, but the state still has no public statewide way to say who is performing the technology labor it buys from private contractors.
